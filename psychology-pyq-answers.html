Unit I

Introduction to Psychology

▾
Q1Define Psychology2–5 M▾

Definition

  • Psychology = the scientific study of behaviour and mental processes of individuals
  • From Greek: Psyche (mind/soul) + Logos (study/science)
  • William James: "The science of mental life"
  • J.B. Watson: "The science of behaviour" (Behaviourist view)
  • Hilgard & Atkinson: "Scientific study of behaviour and mental processes of man and other animals"

Key Terms

  • Behaviour: any observable, measurable action (speaking, walking, crying)
  • Mental processes: internal events — thinking, feeling, perceiving, remembering
  • Scientific: uses systematic, objective, empirical methods — not guesswork or tradition

Evolution of the Definition

  • Ancient: study of the soul (philosophy)
  • 1879 — Wundt: study of consciousness (introspection)
  • 1913 — Watson: study of behaviour only (Behaviourism)
  • Modern: behaviour AND mental processes (cognitive revolution)
✍️ Always include "scientific study," both behaviour AND mental processes, and cite at least one scholar.
Q2Explain Scope and Importance of Psychology in Nursing10 M▾

Scope of Psychology (Branches)

  • Clinical Psychology: assessment & treatment of mental disorders
  • Health Psychology: role of behaviour and lifestyle in health & illness
  • Educational Psychology: learning, teaching methods, student behaviour
  • Developmental Psychology: psychological changes across the lifespan
  • Social Psychology: influence of groups and society on behaviour
  • Industrial/Organisational Psychology: workplace motivation, leadership
  • Nursing Psychology: applying all the above in patient care settings

Importance of Psychology in Nursing

  • 1. Understanding Patient Behaviour: recognise and respond therapeutically to anxiety, denial, aggression — instead of reacting punitively
  • 2. Therapeutic Communication: psychological knowledge improves verbal and non-verbal communication with patients and families
  • 3. Holistic Care: illness has psychological dimensions — nurse treats the whole person, not just the disease
  • 4. Pain Management: fear and anxiety amplify pain perception; psychological interventions reduce pain experience
  • 5. Patient Education: learning theories (readiness, motivation, reinforcement) make health teaching more effective
  • 6. Special Groups: children, elderly, chronically ill, dying patients each have unique psychological needs
  • 7. Nurse's Own Mental Health: self-awareness, stress management, and burnout prevention
  • 8. Motivation & Compliance: understanding what motivates patients improves treatment adherence
  • 9. Crisis Intervention: identifying suicidal ideation, acute anxiety, grief reactions
  • 10. Interpersonal Relationships: better nurse–patient, nurse–doctor, nurse–team dynamics
🔑 THPPEMNCKI: Therapeutic, Holistic, Pain, Patient education, Elderly/special groups, Motivation, Communication, Knowledge, Interpersonal, Inner health of nurse
Q3Describe the Methods of Psychology10 M▾

1. Observational Method

  • Naturalistic: watching behaviour in natural settings without interference (e.g., watching nurse–patient interactions in a ward)
  • Advantage: realistic; high ecological validity
  • Disadvantage: no cause-and-effect; observer bias

2. Experimental Method

  • Manipulates Independent Variable (IV) → measures effect on Dependent Variable (DV)
  • Has control group vs experimental group
  • Advantage: ONLY method that establishes cause and effect
  • Disadvantage: artificial lab setting; ethical constraints in medical research

3. Case Study Method

  • In-depth study of a single individual using interviews, records, observation, tests
  • Very useful in nursing for unusual patient presentations
  • Disadvantage: cannot generalise to all people

4. Survey / Interview Method

  • Questionnaires or structured/unstructured interviews to gather large data sets
  • Used to assess attitudes, mental health prevalence, patient satisfaction
  • Disadvantage: social desirability bias — responses may not reflect true behaviour

5. Introspection

  • Self-examination of one's own thoughts and sensations
  • Introduced by Wilhelm Wundt (1879 — first psychology lab)
  • Limitation: highly subjective; not verifiable by others

6. Clinical / Psychoanalytic Method

  • Free association, dream analysis, projective tests — uncovers unconscious material
  • Relevant in psychiatric nursing assessment

7. Psychological Tests

  • Standardised instruments — IQ tests (WAIS), personality (MMPI), projective (Rorschach)
  • Administer, score, and interpret to give objective data
🔑 OECSI PT: Observation, Experiment, Case study, Survey, Introspection, Psychological Tests

Q3Define Sensation and Explain Sensory Processes — Normal and Abnormal10 M▾

Definition of Sensation

  • Sensation = the process by which sense organs detect and transmit physical stimuli from the environment to the brain
  • Pathway: Stimulus → Receptor → Transduction (nerve impulse) → Brain

Types of Sensory Processes

  • Exteroceptive: receives external stimuli — Vision, Hearing, Smell, Taste, Touch (pressure, pain, temperature)
  • Interoceptive: internal organs — hunger, thirst, nausea, visceral pain
  • Proprioceptive: body position and movement — muscles, joints, balance (vestibular)

Normal Sensory Processes

  • Accurate transmission of real stimuli via intact sense organs
  • Appropriate thresholds: Absolute threshold (minimum to detect) and Difference threshold / JND (minimum change detectable)
  • Sensory adaptation: reduced sensitivity to constant stimuli (e.g., not noticing your own perfume after a while)

Abnormal Sensory Processes

  • Hyperesthesia: abnormally increased sensitivity (e.g., extreme light sensitivity in migraine)
  • Hypoesthesia: reduced sensitivity (e.g., diabetic peripheral neuropathy)
  • Anesthesia: complete absence of sensation
  • Paresthesia: abnormal sensations without adequate stimulus — tingling, burning, "pins and needles"
  • Hallucination: perception with NO external stimulus — auditory in schizophrenia, visual in delirium
  • Illusion: misinterpretation of a real stimulus

Nursing Implications

  • Assess for sensory deficits in elderly, diabetic, neurological patients
  • Modify environment to compensate for sensory loss (adequate lighting, clear speech)
  • Never dismiss abnormal sensory reports — investigate underlying cause
  • Hallucinating patients need safety monitoring and psychiatric referral
Q4Factors Affecting Attention / Determinants of Attention5–10 M▾

Definition of Attention

  • Selective focusing of consciousness on a particular stimulus while excluding others
  • William James: "Everyone knows what attention is… taking possession of the mind in clear and vivid form"

A. Objective Factors (Properties of the Stimulus)

  • Intensity: loud sounds, bright lights attract more attention (e.g., loud ward alarm)
  • Size: larger stimuli get more attention
  • Novelty: new/unusual stimuli attract attention over familiar ones
  • Movement: moving objects attract attention over stationary ones
  • Repetition: repeated stimuli draw attention (initially)
  • Contrast: stimuli standing out from background (e.g., coloured warning text)

B. Subjective Factors (Properties of the Person)

  • Interest: we attend best to what interests us
  • Motivation / Need: hungry person notices food-related stimuli more
  • Mental Set: preparedness to attend to specific stimuli
  • Emotion: emotionally charged stimuli get priority; anxiety narrows attention
  • Fatigue: reduces capacity for sustained attention — important in nursing shift work
  • Practice: experienced nurses notice abnormal signs that novices miss
  • Health status: pain, fever, anxiety reduce attentional capacity

Nursing Applications

  • Use novel, interesting teaching materials to capture patient attention
  • Minimise distractions during patient education and consent
  • Assess patient's attentional capacity before starting any teaching
🔑 Objective: INSMC — Intensity, Novelty, Size, Movement, Contrast
Subjective: IMSEF — Interest, Motivation, Set, Emotion, Fatigue
Q5Principles / Characteristics of Perception5–10 M▾

Definition

  • Perception = the active brain process of organising, interpreting, and giving meaning to sensory information using prior experience and current context

Principles / Characteristics of Perception

  • 1. Selectivity: we cannot process all stimuli; brain selects the most relevant (Figure-Ground principle)
  • 2. Organisation: brain automatically groups stimuli into patterns — Gestalt laws (Proximity, Similarity, Closure, Continuity)
  • 3. Constancy: objects appear stable despite changing sensory input — Size constancy, Shape constancy, Colour constancy
  • 4. Meaningfulness: every perception carries meaning shaped by past experience
  • 5. Subjectivity: two people perceive the same event differently based on experience and emotion
  • 6. Context-dependence: same stimulus perceived differently in different contexts
  • 7. Set (Expectation): what we expect to perceive, we often do — perceptual set guides interpretation
  • 8. Active process: perception is not passive recording — brain actively constructs it
🔑 SOCMSCA: Selectivity, Organisation, Constancy, Meaningfulness, Subjectivity, Context, Active

Q1Explain Self-Actualization and Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs10 M▾

Maslow's Theory — Introduction

  • Abraham Maslow (1943) — "A Theory of Human Motivation" — Humanistic theory
  • Human needs are arranged in a hierarchy; lower needs must be met before higher needs become motivating

The Five Levels (Bottom → Top)

  • Level 1 — Physiological: food, water, oxygen, sleep, warmth — most basic; if unmet, nothing else matters
  • Level 2 — Safety: physical safety, stability, freedom from fear — patients fear pain, procedures, death
  • Level 3 — Love/Belonging: affection, friendship, sense of belonging — hospitalization causes intense isolation; these needs spike
  • Level 4 — Esteem: self-respect, achievement, recognition — illness threatens these; patient feels helpless, ashamed of dependency
  • Level 5 — Self-Actualization: realizing one's full potential, creativity, peak experiences — highest level; only ~1% reach it

Self-Actualization — Detailed

  • Characteristics (Maslow): realistic perception, autonomous, creative, spontaneous, deep relationships, peak experiences, problem-centred, acceptance of self and others, sense of humour
  • Examples: Gandhi, Einstein, Mother Teresa, Abraham Lincoln

Nursing Application

  • Prioritise physiological needs FIRST (airway, breathing, circulation) in acute illness
  • Address safety needs — explain procedures to reduce fear
  • Support belonging — allow family, maintain therapeutic relationship
  • Preserve esteem — involve in decisions, maintain dignity
  • Support self-actualization in rehabilitation — encourage meaningful activities
  • Sickness = regression to lower levels; recovery = ascending the pyramid
🔑 "Please Stop Loving Everyone Selflessly" — Physiological, Safety, Love, Esteem, Self-Actualization
✍️ Draw and label the pyramid. Apply each level to a nursing scenario for full marks.
Q2Write Any Theory of Motivation5 M▾

Drive Reduction Theory (Hull, 1943)

  • When a biological need arises, it creates a drive (tension state) that motivates behaviour to reduce that drive
  • Need → Drive → Behaviour → Drive Reduction (Goal achieved) → Homeostasis restored
  • Example: Thirst (need) → drink water (behaviour) → quench thirst (drive reduction)
  • Limitation: does not explain behaviours that increase arousal (bungee jumping, spicy food)

McClelland's Achievement Motivation Theory

  • Three social motives: Need for Achievement (n-Ach), Need for Affiliation (n-Aff), Need for Power (n-Pow)
  • n-Ach people: prefer moderate challenges, immediate feedback, personal responsibility
  • Nursing: nurses with high n-Ach strive for excellence; high n-Aff nurses are more empathic
✍️ If asked "write any theory" — use Maslow's (most complete) or Drive Reduction (simplest to explain quickly).
Q3Define Motivation — Biological and Social Motives10 M▾

Definition

  • Motivation = the internal driving force that initiates, directs, and sustains goal-directed behaviour
  • From Latin movere = to move
  • Chain: Need (deficiency) → Drive (tension) → Behaviour (goal-directed) → Goal (need satisfied)

Biological (Primary / Physiological) Motives

  • Innate, unlearned, have a clear physiological/homeostatic basis
  • Hunger: blood glucose drop → hypothalamus → eating
  • Thirst: osmoreceptors → dehydration signal → drinking
  • Sleep: adenosine accumulation → sleep drive; circadian rhythm
  • Sex: hormonal (testosterone, oestrogen)
  • Pain avoidance: reflex-based protective drive
  • Temperature regulation: shivering / sweating homeostatic drives
  • Maternal drive: instinct to protect offspring

Social (Secondary / Psychological) Motives

  • Learned, culturally shaped, vary between individuals and societies
  • Achievement (n-Ach — McClelland): desire to excel and succeed
  • Affiliation: need to belong; intensifies during illness
  • Power: desire to influence and control others
  • Security: need for stability and safety
  • Approval: need for recognition and praise
  • Curiosity: drive to explore and know — basis of learning

Nursing Relevance

  • Illness disrupts biological motives: anorexia (lost hunger drive), insomnia
  • Social motives intensify during hospitalisation: affiliation (need family), security (need reassurance)
  • Non-compliance often reflects a motivational deficit — address the underlying need
Q4Explain Frustration and Conflict: Causes and Effects + Conflict Resolution10 M▾

Frustration — Definition and Causes

  • Frustration = the emotional state arising when motivated behaviour is blocked or thwarted, preventing the person from reaching their goal
  • External causes: physical obstacles, other people, hospital rules, circumstances
  • Internal causes: personal limitations (disease, disability, lack of skill)
  • In patients: pain, immobility, inability to perform usual roles = major frustration sources

Effects / Responses to Frustration

  • Aggression: outward hostility — Dollard's Frustration-Aggression Hypothesis; patient shouts at nurse
  • Regression: returning to childlike behaviour (adult cries, becomes dependent)
  • Withdrawal: social withdrawal, apathy, depression
  • Fixation: repeating the same ineffective behaviour rigidly
  • Constructive coping: problem-solving, finding alternative routes — healthy response
  • Displacement: redirecting frustration onto another person or object

Conflict — Definition and Types (Lewin)

  • Conflict = simultaneous presence of two or more incompatible goals creating psychological tension
  • Approach–Approach: two equally attractive goals; pleasant dilemma. Ex: two good treatment options
  • Avoidance–Avoidance: two equally unpleasant choices; double bind. Ex: surgery vs untreated pain
  • Approach–Avoidance: one goal has both attractive AND repulsive aspects. Ex: patient wants recovery but fears chemotherapy
  • Double Approach–Avoidance: multiple goals each with pros and cons; most common in real life. Ex: dialysis vs transplant — both have advantages and risks

Techniques of Conflict Resolution

  • Negotiation: both parties discuss and reach a mutually acceptable solution
  • Compromise: each side concedes something
  • Mediation: neutral third party helps resolve dispute
  • Counselling: therapist helps individual resolve internal conflict
  • Prioritisation: rank goals by importance to clarify choices
  • Reframing: changing perspective to see the situation differently
  • Problem-solving: systematic, creative search for solutions
🔑 Four conflict types: App-App | Avoid-Avoid | App-Avoid | Double App-Avoid

Emotions

▾
Q1Define Emotions and Explain Expression and Control of Emotions10 M▾

Definition

  • Emotion = a complex psychological state involving subjective feeling + physiological arousal + cognitive appraisal + expressive behaviour
  • From Latin emovere = to stir up, to move
  • Paul Ekman's 6 universal basic emotions: Fear, Anger, Disgust, Happiness, Sadness, Surprise

Four Components of Emotion

  • Subjective feeling: conscious awareness — "I feel afraid"
  • Physiological arousal: ↑HR, ↑BP, pupil dilation, sweating, adrenaline — controlled by amygdala and ANS
  • Cognitive appraisal: interpretation of the situation (Lazarus — appraisal determines emotion)
  • Behavioural expression: facial expression, posture, speech, actions

Theories of Emotion

  • James–Lange: physiological change PRECEDES emotion ("we feel afraid because our heart races")
  • Cannon–Bard: emotion and physiology occur SIMULTANEOUSLY via the thalamus
  • Schachter–Singer (Two-Factor): arousal + cognitive label = emotion (context determines which emotion)
  • Lazarus Cognitive Appraisal: how you interpret the situation determines the emotion

Expression of Emotions

  • Facial expressions: universal across cultures (Ekman) — smile, frown, raised brows
  • Body language: posture, gestures, personal space, touch
  • Vocal: tone, pitch, speed of speech, silence
  • Physiological: blushing, trembling, sweating (involuntary)
  • Verbal: direct expression, poetry, art, journaling

Control / Regulation of Emotions

  • Cognitive reappraisal: reinterpreting the situation to change emotional response
  • Relaxation techniques: deep breathing, PMR (progressive muscle relaxation), meditation
  • Physical activity: releases endorphins, reduces cortisol
  • Social support: sharing emotions with trusted others
  • Problem-solving: addressing the source of negative emotion directly
  • Healthy expression: art, music, journaling, therapy — not suppression
  • Mindfulness: observing emotions without judgment

Emotions in Sickness — Nursing Relevance

  • Most common emotions in sick patients: anxiety, fear, anger, depression, denial, guilt, hope
  • Kübler-Ross DABDA: Denial → Anger → Bargaining → Depression → Acceptance
  • Nurse's role: active listening, empathy, allow expression, explain procedures, refer to counsellor when needed

Personality

Q1Define Personality and Determinants of Personality10 M▾

Definition

  • Personality = the unique, relatively stable pattern of thoughts, feelings, and behaviours that characterises an individual and distinguishes them from all others
  • Gordon Allport: "the dynamic organisation within the individual of those psychophysical systems that determine their unique adjustments to the environment"
  • From Latin persona = theatrical mask; psychology uses it to mean the true self

Freud's Structural Theory

  • Id: primitive, unconscious, Pleasure Principle — seeks immediate gratification; present from birth
  • Ego: conscious mediator, Reality Principle — delays gratification, finds realistic solutions
  • Superego: internalized morality, Morality Principle — conscience and ideal self

Determinants of Personality

  • 1. Biological/Genetic: temperament, physical constitution, brain chemistry (serotonin, dopamine), genetic disorders
  • 2. Family Environment: parenting style (authoritative → best outcomes), sibling position, family values — shapes earliest personality
  • 3. Cultural Factors: cultural norms, religion, roles determine which traits are encouraged or suppressed
  • 4. Peer Group: especially powerful in adolescence — conformity, social comparison
  • 5. School and Education: teachers as role models; academic success/failure shapes self-concept
  • 6. Socioeconomic Status: poverty vs affluence shapes aspirations, coping styles, values
  • 7. Life Experiences: trauma, achievements, losses — significant events reshape personality
  • 8. Physical Health: chronic illness alters personality through pain, dependency, body image changes
🔑 BFCPSEL-H: Biological, Family, Culture, Peers, School, Economy, Life experiences, Health
Q2Describe Projective Techniques of Personality Assessment10 M▾

Definition

  • Projective tests present ambiguous stimuli onto which the person projects their unconscious thoughts, feelings, and personality
  • Based on Freud's concept of projection; yield rich qualitative data about personality and unconscious conflicts

Major Projective Tests

  • 1. Rorschach Inkblot Test (Hermann Rorschach, 1921): 10 symmetrical inkblot cards (5 black-white, 2 black-red, 3 coloured); patient describes what they see; scored for location, determinant (color/form), content (human/animal), popularity; reveals unconscious thoughts and emotional states
  • 2. Thematic Apperception Test (TAT — Murray, 1935): 20 ambiguous picture cards of people in situations; patient tells a story for each card; stories reveal dominant needs, motives, conflicts, interpersonal patterns; heroes in stories = patient's own wishes and fears
  • 3. Sentence Completion Test: incomplete sentences (e.g., "I feel most afraid when…"); patient completes in own words; fast, easy; reveals attitudes and hidden concerns
  • 4. Draw-a-Person Test (DAP): patient draws a person, then opposite sex; details analysed (size, omissions, placement, line pressure); especially useful with children
  • 5. Word Association Test (Jung): examiner says a word; patient responds with first word that comes to mind; long delays or unusual responses indicate complexes/conflicts

Advantages

  • Reveals unconscious material that questionnaires cannot access
  • Resistant to faking (unlike self-report scales)
  • Rich, holistic picture of personality

Limitations

  • Scoring is subjective — lower inter-rater reliability
  • Time consuming; requires trained psychologist
  • Cultural bias in test stimuli
✍️ Rorschach and TAT are the most frequently tested. Know: inventor, year, materials, what it measures.
Q3Explain Personality Attributes of a Nurse / Personality in Nursing5–10 M▾

Ideal Personality Attributes of a Nurse

  • Empathy: understand and share patient feelings without being overwhelmed; foundation of therapeutic relationship
  • Emotional Stability: calm in emergencies; manages own emotions professionally; doesn't become overwhelmed by patient distress
  • Patience: especially with elderly, intellectually disabled, terminally ill, or difficult patients
  • Non-judgmental Attitude: accepts patients regardless of diagnosis (addiction, HIV, mental illness, lifestyle)
  • Honesty and Integrity: truthful with patients and colleagues; professional ethics upheld at all times
  • Self-Awareness: recognises own biases, values, emotional reactions — prevents these from harming patient care
  • Adaptability: flexible in rapidly changing clinical situations
  • Conscientiousness: meticulous and detail-oriented — critical for medication administration and documentation
  • Strong Communication Skills: clear, compassionate, culturally sensitive verbal and non-verbal communication
  • Resilience: recovers from stressful situations, patient loss, difficult shifts
  • High EQ (Emotional Intelligence — Goleman): self-awareness, self-regulation, empathy, motivation, social skills

Personality in Sickness — How Illness Affects Personality

  • Illness stresses existing personality patterns and exaggerates pre-morbid traits
  • Regression: adult becomes dependent, childlike
  • Type A patient: resists sick role, hostile, non-compliant — hard to nurse
  • Type B patient: relaxed, compliant — easier nursing relationship
  • Paranoid personality: suspicious of medications and nurses — needs extra trust-building
🔑 EPNHSA-CCRL: Empathy, Patience, Non-judgmental, Honesty, Self-awareness, Adaptable, Conscientious, Communication, Resilience, Leadership

Psychological Needs — Developmental

Q1Discuss Psychological Needs of Children During Illness10 M▾

Three Major Stressors for Hospitalised Children

  • 1. Separation from parents — greatest fear; Bowlby's attachment theory
  • 2. Loss of control — unfamiliar environment, forced procedures
  • 3. Bodily injury and pain — fear of pain, mutilation, death

Needs by Age Group

  • Infants (0–1 yr): need consistent primary caregiver (bonding); stranger anxiety peaks at ~8 months; need touch, warmth, feeding routine; separation from mother = extreme distress — Erikson: Trust vs Mistrust
  • Toddlers (1–3 yr): ritualistic routines essential; resist procedures; regression to earlier behaviour is normal (bedwetting, thumb-sucking); brief simple explanations only — Erikson: Autonomy vs Shame
  • Preschool (3–6 yr): magical thinking — may believe illness is punishment for wrongdoing; fear of body mutilation; use therapeutic play — Erikson: Initiative vs Guilt
  • School age (6–12 yr): fear of being "different"; wants to understand procedures; capable of reasoning; concerned about missing school; needs sense of mastery — Erikson: Industry vs Inferiority

Core Psychological Needs

  • Security: consistent caregivers, familiar objects (favourite toy), predictable routines
  • Parental presence: rooming-in for parents; minimise separation — distress is proportional to separation
  • Age-appropriate information: honest, simple — "Your blood is being tested to help you feel better"
  • Play therapy: allows processing of fears; medical play — examining doll with stethoscope
  • Control: offer choices wherever possible — "Left arm or right arm?" — preserves autonomy
  • Adequate pain management: children's pain is chronically undertreated

Nursing Interventions

  • Assign primary nurse — minimise number of different caregivers
  • Use distraction during painful procedures (blowing bubbles, music, storytelling)
  • Never perform painful procedures while parent is present — parent = safe person
  • Teach parents caregiving skills to reduce their anxiety and maintain competence
Q2Discuss Psychological Needs of Adolescents10 M▾

Erikson's Stage: Identity vs Role Confusion (12–18 yrs)

  • Core task: forming a stable identity — "Who am I?"
  • Illness disrupts this process and threatens identity formation

Key Psychological Needs

  • Body image and self-esteem: extremely sensitive to physical changes; hair loss, scars, weight changes, colostomy = devastating. Nurse must proactively address body image concerns
  • Independence and autonomy: resist being treated as children; must be involved in care decisions; always provide rationale for every procedure
  • Privacy and confidentiality: absolute; discuss care privately; do not discuss in front of parents without patient's consent
  • Peer relationships: fear of being "different" or "left behind"; maintain social connection (phone, social media within limits)
  • Education continuity: worried about missing school and exams; coordinate with school; hospital teachers where available
  • Full honest information: want complete explanations, not watered-down versions; can understand medical facts if explained clearly
  • Trust: must trust the nurse personally before accepting care
  • Sexual identity: illness raises sexuality concerns; nurse must be comfortable addressing these sensitively

Nursing Approach

  • Negotiate care — do not dictate; use shared decision-making
  • Maintain confidentiality — this builds trust faster than anything else
  • Peer support groups for chronic conditions (e.g., diabetes camps, cancer peer support)
  • Use relatable language — avoid both childish and overly technical terms
Q3Discuss Psychological Needs of the Elderly During Illness10 M▾

Erikson's Stage: Ego Integrity vs Despair (65+ yrs)

  • Successful aging = looking back with satisfaction (Ego Integrity)
  • Failure = despair, regret, bitterness
  • Illness and hospitalisation can tip the balance toward despair

Psychological Challenges

  • Fear of death and dying: illness makes death feel imminent; need to discuss fears openly and respectfully
  • Loss of independence: most feared — inability to perform ADLs is deeply distressing; loss of control
  • Cognitive changes: memory loss, delirium ("sundowning"), early dementia — nurse must differentiate delirium (acute, reversible) from dementia (chronic, progressive)
  • Social isolation: loss of spouse and friends; reduced mobility limits social contact
  • Multiple losses: bereavement, financial losses, role losses (retirement) — accumulated grief
  • Sensory deficits: hearing loss → misunderstanding → frustration; vision loss → falls, anxiety
  • Depression: most common and most undertreated mental health issue in elderly — screen with GDS (Geriatric Depression Scale)
  • Ageism: feeling dismissed, ignored, or talked down to — profound negative effect on self-esteem

Nursing Interventions

  • Speak clearly, face-to-face, at normal pace (shouting is disrespectful and distorts speech)
  • Maintain orientation — clock, calendar, daily orientation conversations
  • Maximise independence — allow self-care wherever safe
  • Reminiscence therapy — structured life review improves psychological wellbeing
  • Involve family; assess and support caregiver burden
  • Spiritual support — respect religious practices and end-of-life wishes
  • Fall prevention — adequate lighting, call bell, non-slip footwear
  • Respect experience and wisdom — never patronise

Individual Differences

Q1Define Individual Differences and Their Significance in Nursing10 M▾

Definition

  • Individual differences = the variations in physical, psychological, intellectual, social, and emotional characteristics that make each person unique
  • No two individuals — even identical twins — are psychologically identical
  • Pioneer: Francis Galton

Areas of Individual Differences

  • Intelligence and cognitive abilities; personality traits; physical characteristics
  • Emotional reactivity and temperament; attitudes and values; coping styles
  • Health behaviour; language and communication style; learning pace and style

Significance in Nursing

  • 1. Individualised Care Plans: not one-size-fits-all; tailor every plan to the specific patient
  • 2. Communication Adaptation: adjust language, pace, and style based on education level, age, culture
  • 3. Pain Assessment: pain threshold varies enormously; use validated tools; do not stereotype
  • 4. Patient Education: adapt content, literacy level, language, and method to individual learner
  • 5. Drug Response: pharmacogenomics — genetic differences affect drug metabolism and side effects
  • 6. Cultural Competence: beliefs about illness, acceptable treatment, family roles in care vary by culture
  • 7. Compliance Assessment: different patients have different reasons for non-compliance — no single solution fits all
  • 8. Nursing Education: nursing students learn differently — teaching must accommodate individual learning styles
Q2Explain Role of Heredity in Individual Differences5–10 M▾

Definition of Heredity

  • Heredity = the transmission of biological traits and characteristics from parents to offspring through genes on chromosomes
  • Humans have 46 chromosomes (23 pairs); 50% from each parent

Role in Individual Differences

  • Physical traits: height, weight, eye colour, skin colour, facial features — largely genetic
  • Temperament: basic emotional reactivity (easy, difficult, slow-to-warm — Thomas & Chess) is largely inborn and persists across life
  • Intelligence: ~50% of IQ variance is genetic; identical twins more similar than fraternal twins even raised apart
  • Personality: Big Five traits (especially neuroticism, extraversion) ~40–60% heritable
  • Mental disorders: schizophrenia (80% heritability), bipolar disorder, depression, autism — strong genetic component
  • Disease susceptibility: diabetes, hypertension, cancer — genetic predisposition

Evidence

  • Twin studies: MZ twins more similar than DZ twins even when raised apart
  • Adoption studies: adopted children more similar to biological parents than adoptive parents in many traits

Nature + Nurture Interaction (Modern View)

  • NOT either/or — both interact continuously
  • Genes set a range of potential; environment determines where within that range
  • Epigenetics: environment can switch genes on or off
  • Example: genetic predisposition to depression + stressful environment triggers it; supportive environment may prevent it
Q3Explain Implications of Individual Differences in Nursing5 M▾

Key Nursing Implications

  • Assessment: comprehensive, individualised assessment of every patient — physical, psychological, social, cultural dimensions
  • Care Planning: tailor interventions to the individual; avoid standardised, routine approaches
  • Communication: match communication style to patient's education, language, culture, and cognitive level
  • Health Education: identify learning style, readiness, and literacy level before teaching
  • Pain Management: use validated scales; don't compare patients' pain responses or apply stereotypes
  • Cultural Sensitivity: understand cultural beliefs about illness, diet, gender roles in care, spiritual needs
  • Non-Judgmental Care: different doesn't mean wrong; individual differences are normal human variation
  • Staff Training: nurses must be trained to recognise and respond to individual differences — cultural competence training, communication skills
✍️ The CORE message: individual differences demand individualised care. "One size fits all" nursing is poor nursing.
Unit VIII

Intelligence & Abilities

▾
Q1Define Intelligence and Write Theories of Intelligence10 M▾

Definition

  • Intelligence = the ability to learn from experience, solve problems, and adapt effectively to new situations
  • Wechsler: "the global capacity of the individual to act purposefully, think rationally, and deal effectively with the environment"

1. Spearman's Two-Factor Theory (1904)

  • 'g' factor: general intelligence — overall mental energy underlying all intellectual activities
  • 's' factor: specific abilities for particular tasks (musical, verbal, mathematical)
  • Smart in one area → tend to be smart in others (g factor)

2. Thurstone's Primary Mental Abilities (1938)

  • 7 independent abilities: Verbal comprehension, Word fluency, Number facility, Spatial ability, Associative memory, Perceptual speed, Reasoning
  • Rejected single 'g' — intelligence is multifaceted

3. Gardner's Multiple Intelligences (1983)

  • 8 intelligences: Linguistic, Logical-mathematical, Spatial, Musical, Bodily-kinesthetic, Interpersonal, Intrapersonal, Naturalist
  • Nursing relevance: high Interpersonal (understanding others) and Intrapersonal (self-awareness) intelligences are essential
  • Each person has a unique profile — traditional IQ captures only a slice

4. Sternberg's Triarchic Theory (1985)

  • Analytical: academic problem-solving — traditional IQ
  • Creative: dealing with novel situations; innovation
  • Practical: "street smarts" — adapting to real-world environments
  • Practical intelligence is highly relevant to nursing; traditional tests miss it

5. Emotional Intelligence / EQ (Goleman, 1995)

  • Self-awareness, Self-regulation, Motivation, Empathy, Social skills
  • Goleman: EQ is more important than IQ for success in caring professions
  • High-EQ nurses → better patient outcomes, less burnout, better team dynamics
✍️ Cover at least 4 theories. For each: name + year + core idea + nursing relevance. Always mention EQ in nursing exams.

  • Formula: IQ = (Mental Age ÷ Chronological Age) × 100
  • Average IQ = 100; Normal range = 85–115; Average category = 90–110

Unit IX

Learning

▾
Q1Describe Trial and Error Theory of Learning (Thorndike)10 M▾

Introduction

  • Proposed by Edward L. Thorndike (1898) — also called Connectionism
  • Experiment: hungry cats placed in puzzle boxes; food outside; had to pull a lever to escape
  • Cats tried random responses until they accidentally pulled the lever; with each trial, time to escape decreased → learning by trial and error

Key Features

  • Learning occurs through random attempts at solving a problem
  • Incorrect responses gradually eliminated; correct response (leading to reward) is strengthened ("stamped in")
  • Learning curve: slow initially, speeds up with practice
  • Motivation required: animal/person must want the goal

Thorndike's THREE Primary Laws

  • Law of Readiness: a learner who is ready to act finds satisfaction in doing so; forcing learning on an unready learner causes frustration. Nursing: always assess patient readiness before teaching
  • Law of Exercise (Use/Disuse): connections strengthened by use (practice); weakened by disuse — "use it or lose it." Nursing: repeat instructions; use return demonstrations; practice clinical skills in lab
  • Law of Effect: responses followed by a satisfying outcome (reward) are stamped in; responses followed by annoying outcomes are stamped out. This is the basis of all reinforcement. Nursing: praise correct technique; give positive feedback after compliant behaviour

Secondary Laws

  • Multiple Response: organism tries many responses until one works
  • Set/Attitude: mental attitude influences which connections form
  • Partial Activity: organism responds selectively to relevant parts of situation
  • Assimilation: new situations responded to like old similar ones
  • Associative Shifting: any stimulus occurring with a response can eventually produce that response alone

Nursing Applications

  • Patient learning insulin injection or dressing change follows trial-and-error — expect initial errors; reinforce correct technique
  • Use positive reinforcement consistently (praise, information feedback)
  • Assess readiness — patient in acute pain is NOT ready to learn
🔑 REE: Readiness, Exercise, Effect — Thorndike's 3 primary laws. Memorise these three.
Q2Define Learning and Principles of Learning10 M▾

Definition

  • Learning = a relatively permanent change in behaviour or its potential that results from practice or experience, excluding changes due to maturation, fatigue, drugs, or injury
  • Key qualifiers: "relatively permanent" + "practice or experience"

Principles / Conditions of Learning

  • 1. Motivation: learner must WANT to learn; intrinsic motivation > extrinsic; without motivation, no learning occurs
  • 2. Readiness: physical, mental, and emotional preparedness; forcing learning on an unready person is futile and distressing
  • 3. Active Participation: doing > watching > listening; return demonstration confirms learning
  • 4. Practice and Repetition: strengthens learning connections; spaced repetition > massed practice for retention
  • 5. Feedback / Knowledge of Results: learner must know immediately whether their response is correct; immediate feedback is most effective
  • 6. Meaningful Content: learning is faster and more durable when content is relevant and useful to the learner
  • 7. Appropriate Pace: not too fast (overwhelm) or too slow (boredom); adjusted to individual rate
  • 8. Pleasant Environment: free from distractions, anxiety-provoking elements; comfortable setting
  • 9. Transfer of Learning: applying previously learned material to new situations; nurse uses anatomy knowledge to explain a procedure
  • 10. Whole vs Part Learning: simple material — teach as whole; complex — break into parts then integrate
🔑 MR-APFM-PETP: Motivation, Readiness, Active, Practice, Feedback, Meaningful, Pace, Environment, Transfer, Parts/whole
✍️ In patient teaching: always assess READINESS and MOTIVATION first. These are the two most tested principles in nursing exams.
Unit X

Memory & Forgetting

▾
Q1Define Memory and Describe the Process of Memory5–10 M▾

Definition

  • Memory = the mental capacity to encode, store, and retrieve information and experiences
  • Memory is the foundation of all learning — without memory, nothing can be retained or applied

Three Stages / Process of Memory

  • 1. Encoding: converting information into a form the brain can process and store; types: acoustic (sound), visual (image), semantic (meaning — deepest and most durable)
  • 2. Storage: retaining encoded information over time in memory systems; quality depends on depth of encoding and emotional significance
  • 3. Retrieval: accessing stored information when needed; types: recall (free recall, cued recall) and recognition; retrieval depends on appropriate cues

Atkinson–Shiffrin Modal Model

  • Sensory Memory: 0.25–3 sec; holds exact sensory impression briefly; Iconic (visual) / Echoic (auditory); fades unless attended to
  • Short-Term Memory (STM) / Working Memory: 15–30 sec; capacity = 7±2 chunks (Miller's Magic Number, 1956); lost without rehearsal
  • Long-Term Memory (LTM): potentially unlimited capacity and duration; organised semantically

Types of Long-Term Memory

  • Explicit/Declarative: consciously recalled
    • Episodic: personal autobiographical events with time and context
    • Semantic: general knowledge and facts (no personal context)
  • Implicit/Non-declarative: unconscious, automatic
    • Procedural: skills and habits — survives even in severe amnesia
    • Priming: prior exposure influences later response
🔑 ESR: Encode, Store, Retrieve — the three stages of memory

Q3Explain Types of Memory and Ways to Improve Memorization10 M▾

Ways to Improve Memorization

  • Elaborative rehearsal: connect new material to existing knowledge (deep processing) — far better than rote repetition
  • Spaced repetition: review at increasing intervals (Day 1 → 3 → 7 → 30) — combats Ebbinghaus forgetting curve (60–70% forgotten in 24 hrs)
  • Mnemonics: acronyms, rhymes, peg systems, method of loci — create memorable retrieval cues
  • Chunking: group individual items into meaningful larger units (e.g., phone number blocks)
  • Visual imagery: create vivid mental pictures for abstract concepts
  • Active recall (testing): testing yourself is more effective than re-reading (retrieval practice effect)
  • Adequate sleep: memory consolidation occurs during sleep — study before sleeping aids retention
  • Minimise interference: study similar subjects at different times to reduce retroactive/proactive interference
  • Emotional association: emotionally significant material remembered better (amygdala–hippocampal connection)
  • Reduce anxiety: high cortisol impairs hippocampal function → reduces encoding and retrieval
🔑 SMILE: Spaced repetition, Mnemonics, Imagery, Links (elaboration), Examine yourself (active recall)
Q4Write Types of Forgetting5–10 M▾

Definition

  • Forgetting = the inability to recall or recognise previously stored information
  • Ebbinghaus Forgetting Curve: ~60–70% of new material forgotten within 24 hours; rate slows over time

Theories / Types of Forgetting

  • 1. Decay Theory: memory trace fades due to disuse over time — "use it or lose it"; explains STM forgetting well; doesn't explain why old vivid memories remain
  • 2. Interference Theory:
    • Proactive interference: OLD learning interferes with NEW (French → harder to learn Spanish)
    • Retroactive interference: NEW learning interferes with OLD (learning Spanish → harder to recall French)
  • 3. Retrieval Failure: information IS stored but cannot be accessed without appropriate retrieval cue; tip-of-tongue phenomenon; context-dependent memory (recall better in same environment as encoding)
  • 4. Motivated Forgetting / Repression (Freud): unconscious suppression of painful, threatening memories into the unconscious; basis of psychoanalytic therapy
  • 5. Encoding Failure: information never properly encoded into LTM in the first place — not true forgetting; e.g., not remembering a face you barely glanced at

Pathological Forgetting

  • Anterograde Amnesia: cannot form NEW memories after brain injury (hippocampal damage) — forward-facing
  • Retrograde Amnesia: cannot recall events BEFORE the injury — backward-facing
  • Alzheimer's Disease: progressive destruction, beginning with episodic memory
✍️ Anterograde = AFTER injury (can't make new memories). Retrograde = BEFORE injury (lost old memories). This distinction is frequently confused in exams.
Unit XI

Attitudes

▾
Q1Define Attitude and Factors Affecting Attitude Formation10 M▾

Definition

  • Attitude = a learned, relatively stable predisposition to respond consistently (favourably or unfavourably) toward a specific person, object, group, or situation
  • Gordon Allport (1935): "A mental and neural state of readiness, organised through experience, exerting a directive or dynamic influence upon the individual's response"
  • Key features: LEARNED, EVALUATIVE (positive/negative), RELATIVELY STABLE

Three Components (ABC Model)

  • A — Affective (Feeling): emotional response — "I feel anxious about injections"
  • B — Behavioural (Conative): predisposition to act — "I avoid giving injections"
  • C — Cognitive: beliefs and knowledge — "Injections cause pain and can cause complications"

Factors Affecting Attitude Formation

  • 1. Classical Conditioning: pairing a neutral object with a positive/negative stimulus creates attitude; negative hospital experience as child → negative attitude toward hospitals as adult
  • 2. Operant Conditioning: reinforced attitudes are strengthened; punished attitudes weakened
  • 3. Observational Learning: watching parents, peers, media — parent's attitude toward mental illness becomes child's attitude
  • 4. Direct Personal Experience: firsthand encounters powerfully shape attitudes; patient treated kindly → positive attitude toward nursing
  • 5. Culture and Social Norms: family values, religion, community standards shape core attitudes
  • 6. Peer Group Influence: especially powerful in adolescence — conformity, social comparison
  • 7. Mass Media: TV, social media — shaping health attitudes (vaccination hesitancy, body image)
  • 8. Education: formal education can counter prejudicial attitudes and broaden perspectives
  • 9. Personality: authoritarian personalities hold more rigid attitudes; open personalities are more flexible
🔑 ABC: Affective, Behavioural, Cognitive — always structure attitude answers using these three components
Q2Describe the Process of Attitudinal Change5–10 M▾

Why Attitudes Change

  • Attitudes are relatively stable but CAN change through experience, persuasion, group pressure, and personal insight

Processes of Attitude Change

  • 1. Persuasive Communication (Hovland): effectiveness depends on source (credibility, attractiveness), message (strength, emotional vs rational), and audience (existing attitudes, intelligence)
  • 2. Cognitive Dissonance (Festinger, 1957): holding contradictory beliefs creates discomfort → person changes attitude to reduce it. Smoker knows smoking kills → "I'll cut down" or "It won't happen to me" → attitude shifts
  • 3. Learning (CC + OC + Modelling): new associations, reinforcement, and role models change attitudes
  • 4. Social Pressure / Group Influence: majority opinion and conformity pressure (Asch studies) change individual attitudes
  • 5. Direct Experience: positive contact with a previously feared group/situation reduces negative attitude (Contact Theory)
  • 6. Motivational Interviewing (MI): collaborative conversation evoking the person's own reasons for change; highly effective for changing unhealthy behaviours in nursing

Role of Attitudes in Health and Sickness

  • Positive health attitudes → preventive behaviours (exercise, vaccination, screening)
  • Negative attitudes toward hospitals → delayed care-seeking → worse outcomes
  • Stigmatising attitudes toward mental illness → prevents help-seeking
  • Nurse's non-judgmental attitude → patient feels safe → better disclosure → better care
  • Health Belief Model: perceived susceptibility + severity + benefits − barriers = likelihood of health action
✍️ For Role of Attitudes in Health and Sickness — give examples from BOTH sides: patient's attitudes affecting health behaviour AND nurse's attitudes affecting care quality.
Unit XII

Defense Mechanisms & Mental Health

▾
Q1Define Defense Mechanisms — Types with Clinical Examples10 M▾

Definition

  • Defense mechanisms = unconscious psychological strategies used by the ego to protect itself from anxiety arising from unacceptable id impulses, moral demands of the superego, or external threats
  • Developed by Sigmund Freud; elaborated by his daughter Anna Freud
  • They are: automatic, unconscious (person is unaware), and reduce anxiety in the short term
  • Healthy in moderation; pathological when rigid, excessive, or significantly distort reality

Mature / Healthy Defense Mechanisms

  • Sublimation: redirecting unacceptable impulses toward socially acceptable activities. Example: highly aggressive person becomes a competitive athlete or surgeon
  • Altruism: helping others to satisfy own unconscious needs. Example: nurse who was once critically ill becomes a dedicated patient advocate
  • Humour: expressing unacceptable feelings through comedy. Example: doctor jokes about a difficult case to release tension
  • Suppression: CONSCIOUSLY postponing awareness of a problem. Example: nurse decides "I'll deal with this worry after my shift ends"

Neurotic Defense Mechanisms (Most Common — Must Know All)

  • Repression: MOST FUNDAMENTAL — forcibly blocking unacceptable thoughts/memories from conscious awareness into the unconscious. Example: adult has no memory of childhood abuse
  • Rationalization: creating logical-sounding but false justifications ("sour grapes"). Example: patient who missed medications says "Those tablets weren't helping anyway"
  • Projection: attributing own unacceptable feelings to others. Example: person feeling hostile says "Everyone here hates me!" — projecting their own anger outward
  • Reaction Formation: expressing the OPPOSITE of true feeling. Example: nurse who feels hostility toward a difficult patient becomes excessively kind and attentive
  • Displacement: redirecting emotion from threatening target to safer target. Example: nurse scolded by doctor → goes home and argues with spouse
  • Intellectualization: using abstract reasoning to avoid emotional impact. Example: terminal cancer patient reads only medical research papers instead of processing their own grief
  • Denial: refusing to accept a painful external reality. Example: alcoholic insists "I don't have a drinking problem"; newly diagnosed patient says "The tests must be wrong"

Immature Defense Mechanisms

  • Regression: reverting to earlier developmental behaviour under stress. Example: hospitalised adult becomes tearful and helpless; sick child resumes bedwetting
  • Somatization: converting emotional conflicts into physical symptoms. Example: student gets headache before exam; grief expressed as chronic pain
  • Splitting: seeing people/things as all good or all bad — no middle ground. Common in Borderline Personality Disorder

Nursing Relevance

  • Recognise defense mechanisms as adaptive coping — not deliberate dishonesty
  • Patient in denial — do not forcibly break down denial; build trust first; early denial may be protective
  • Angry patient displacing onto nurse — do not take it personally; respond with therapeutic communication
  • Document behaviours factually, not interpretations ("patient refused to discuss diagnosis" not "patient is in denial")
🔑 "Really Rude People Reject Drinking During Intellectual Situations"

Q2Write Characteristics of a Mentally Healthy Person10 M▾

WHO Definition of Mental Health

  • "A state of wellbeing in which the individual realises their own abilities, can cope with the normal stresses of life, can work productively and fruitfully, and is able to make a contribution to their community" — WHO (2001)
  • Mental health ≠ merely absence of mental illness — it is a POSITIVE state of wellbeing

Characteristics of a Mentally Healthy Person

  • 1. Positive and Realistic Self-Concept: accurate, balanced self-perception — neither grandiose nor self-deprecating; accepts both strengths and limitations honestly
  • 2. Reality Orientation: perceives self and world accurately; decisions based on reality, not wishful thinking; distinguishes imagination from reality
  • 3. Effective Coping with Stress: faces difficulties constructively; uses healthy coping (problem-solving, social support); does not rely excessively on defense mechanisms
  • 4. Satisfying Interpersonal Relationships: capable of forming and maintaining mutually satisfying relationships; gives and receives love and affection; genuinely empathic
  • 5. Emotional Balance: experiences the full range of emotions appropriately; neither suppresses all feelings nor loses control; regulates emotions constructively
  • 6. Autonomy and Self-Direction: makes independent decisions; takes responsibility for own behaviour; not overly dependent on others for approval or decisions
  • 7. Productive and Purposeful: engaged in meaningful work, study, or creative activities; contributes to family and society; has a clear sense of purpose
  • 8. Adaptability and Flexibility: adjusts to change; tolerates uncertainty; resilient in the face of adversity
  • 9. Freedom from Excessive Anxiety and Guilt: not paralysed by guilt or anxiety; manages internal conflicts without being overwhelmed
  • 10. Spiritual Wellbeing: sense of meaning, purpose, and connection to something greater; inner peace; not necessarily religious but spiritually grounded
  • 11. Healthy Physical Habits: adequate sleep, nutrition, exercise — recognises and respects the mind–body connection
  • 12. Positive Outlook: optimism; hope for the future; capacity for gratitude and enjoyment

Nurse's Role in Promoting Mental Health

  • Create therapeutic, non-stigmatising ward environment
  • Teach stress management to patients and community
  • Early identification of mental health deterioration — screen routinely
  • Model mentally healthy behaviour — nurse as role model
  • Practise self-care to maintain own mental health and prevent burnout
🔑 PREFACE-AS: Positive self, Reality, Effective coping, Friends, Autonomy, Contributions, Emotions balanced, Adaptability, Spiritual
✍️ Always start with the WHO definition (memorise it word for word). Then give 10–12 characteristics in numbered points. Examiners check for the WHO definition specifically.
