Unit I

Introduction to Psychology

▾
🧠
Definition of Psychology
Unit I
▾

Definition

  • Psychology = "the scientific study of behaviour and mental processes" (APA)
  • Derived from Greek: Psyche (mind/soul) + Logos (study)
  • Earlier defined as "study of the soul/mind"; now operationally defined as study of observable behaviour + internal mental events
  • Behaviour = any observable action (speaking, walking, crying)
  • Mental processes = thoughts, feelings, memories, perceptions (not directly observable)

Key Definitions by Scholars

  • Watson (1913): "Psychology is the science of behaviour" — Behaviourist view
  • William James: "The science of mental life"
  • Hilgard: "Science of behaviour and mental processes of man and other animals"
🔑 Mnemonic: BMP — Behaviour, Mental Processes, People (what psychology studies)
⭐ Exam Tip: Always define psychology as "scientific study" — the word scientific is key. Mention both behaviour AND mental processes.
🔭
Scope & Goals of Psychology
Unit I
▾

Four Main Goals

  • Description: What is happening? (observe & record behaviour)
  • Explanation: Why is it happening? (cause & effect)
  • Prediction: What will happen next?
  • Control/Modification: How can we change or improve behaviour?

Scope / Areas

  • Clinical Psychology: assessment, diagnosis & treatment of mental disorders
  • Health Psychology: role of behaviour in health & illness
  • Educational Psychology: learning, teaching methods
  • Industrial Psychology: workplace behaviour, productivity
  • Social Psychology: influence of society on individual behaviour
  • Developmental Psychology: lifespan changes
  • Nursing Psychology: applying psychology principles in patient care
🔑 DEPC — Describe, Explain, Predict, Control
🔬
Methods of Psychology
Unit I
▾

Observational Methods

  • Naturalistic Observation: studying behaviour in natural setting without interference (e.g., watching patient interactions in ward)
  • Controlled Observation: structured lab setting
  • Participant Observation: researcher joins the group being studied

Experimental Method

  • Manipulates independent variable (IV) → measures dependent variable (DV)
  • Has control & experimental group
  • Most scientific method — establishes cause-effect

Survey / Interview Methods

  • Questionnaires, structured/unstructured interviews
  • Used to gather attitudes, opinions, history

Case Study Method

  • In-depth study of a single individual or group
  • Useful in nursing for understanding unusual presentations

Introspection

  • Self-examination of one's own thought processes
  • Subjective; earliest psychological method (Wundt)

Clinical Method

  • Includes psychological tests, case history, physical examination
  • Most relevant for nurses
🔑 OESCI: Observation, Experiment, Survey, Case study, Introspection

Unit II

Sensation, Attention & Perception

▾
👁️
Sensation — Definition & Sensory Processes
Unit II
▾

Definition

  • Sensation = the process by which sense organs detect physical stimuli from the environment and transmit them to the brain
  • Involves receptor → afferent nerve → brain
  • It is the raw data before interpretation

Types of Sensation (Sense Modalities)

  • Exteroceptive: Vision, Hearing, Touch, Taste, Smell — receives external stimuli
  • Interoceptive: visceral sensations (pain, hunger, nausea)
  • Proprioceptive: position and movement of body parts (muscles, joints)

Sensory Threshold

  • Absolute threshold: minimum stimulus needed to produce sensation 50% of time
  • Difference threshold (JND): minimum change in stimulus that can be detected
  • Sensory adaptation: decreased sensitivity after prolonged exposure (e.g., smell in ICU)

Normal vs Abnormal Sensation

  • Normal: accurate transmission of stimuli via intact sense organs
  • Hyperesthesia: abnormally increased sensitivity (pain)
  • Hypoesthesia/Anesthesia: decreased or absent sensation
  • Paresthesia: abnormal sensations (tingling, burning) without stimulus
  • Hallucination: perception without external stimulus (important in psych nursing)
⭐ Exam: Distinguish sensation from perception. Sensation = detection; Perception = interpretation.
🎯
Attention — Definition & Distraction
Unit II
▾

Definition

  • Attention = selective concentration of consciousness on a particular stimulus while ignoring others
  • It is the focusing of mental resources on selected information
  • William James: "Everyone knows what attention is… taking possession of the mind in clear and vivid form"

Types of Attention

  • Involuntary/Reflexive: automatic, no effort (e.g., loud noise pulls attention)
  • Voluntary/Deliberate: conscious effort (e.g., studying)
  • Habitual: no effort after repetition (e.g., experienced nurse scanning monitor)

Factors Affecting Attention (Determinants)

  • Objective factors (stimulus properties): Intensity, Size, Novelty, Movement, Repetition, Contrast
  • Subjective factors (personal): Interest, Set/Expectation, Emotions, Fatigue, Practice, Needs/Motives

Distraction & Contributory Factors

  • Internal distractors: pain, anxiety, hunger, emotional distress
  • External distractors: noise, movement, bright lights
  • In nursing: patient distraction can affect understanding of instructions, consent
  • Strategy: minimize distractors during patient teaching/counselling
🔑 INSME: Intensity, Novelty, Size, Movement, Emotion — factors that capture attention
🧩
Perception — Normal & Abnormal
Unit II
▾

Definition

  • Perception = the process by which the brain organizes, interprets, and gives meaning to sensory information
  • Goes beyond raw sensation — involves higher brain functions
  • Influenced by past experience, expectations, culture, emotions

Characteristics of Perception

  • Selectivity: we perceive what is relevant to us (figure-ground)
  • Organisation: we group stimuli into patterns (Gestalt principles)
  • Constancy: objects appear stable despite changes in stimulus (size, shape constancy)
  • Meaningfulness: perception is always meaningful/interpretive
  • Subjectivity: two people may perceive same event differently

Gestalt Principles

  • Figure-Ground: focusing on one element vs background
  • Proximity: close objects seen as group
  • Similarity: similar objects grouped together
  • Closure: filling in gaps to make complete picture
  • Continuity: perceiving smooth flowing lines

Normal Perception

  • Accurate interpretation matching reality
  • Consistent with others' perception of same stimulus

Abnormal Perception

  • Illusion: misinterpretation of a real external stimulus (e.g., rope seen as snake)
  • Hallucination: perception without any external stimulus; most serious perceptual abnormality
  • Types of hallucination: Auditory (most common in schizophrenia), Visual, Olfactory, Gustatory, Tactile
  • Depersonalization: feeling detached from one's own body
  • Derealization: surroundings seem unreal
⭐ Important: Illusion = real stimulus misinterpreted. Hallucination = NO real stimulus. This distinction is frequently tested.
Unit III

Motivation

▾
🚀
Definition & Nature of Motivation
Unit III
▾

Definition

  • Motivation = the driving force that initiates, directs, and sustains goal-directed behaviour
  • From Latin movere = to move
  • Includes motives (internal states) that push/pull behaviour toward goals
  • Motivation = Needs + Drives + Incentives

Nature/Characteristics

  • It is an internal state/condition
  • It is goal-directed
  • It energizes and sustains behaviour
  • Can be conscious or unconscious
  • Can be biological or learned (social)
  • It is dynamic — changes with time and situation

Key Concepts

  • Need: deficiency state (e.g., hunger = need for food)
  • Drive: internal tension arising from need (motivates action)
  • Incentive: external object/event that motivates (e.g., food, money, praise)
  • Goal: what satisfies the drive
🔑 NDIG: Need → Drive → Incentive → Goal
🧬
Biological & Social Motives
Unit III
▾

Biological (Primary) Motives

  • Innate, unlearned, physiological basis
  • Hunger: blood glucose drop → hypothalamus → eating behaviour
  • Thirst: osmoreceptors in hypothalamus detect dehydration
  • Sleep: RAS (Reticular Activating System) regulation
  • Sex/Reproduction: hormonal drive
  • Pain avoidance: protective reflex mechanism
  • Temperature regulation: homeostatic drive
  • Maternal drive: care for offspring

Social (Secondary/Psychological) Motives

  • Learned, culturally influenced
  • Achievement motive: desire to excel (McClelland — n-Ach)
  • Affiliation motive: need to belong, be with others
  • Power motive: desire to influence others
  • Approval motive: need for recognition/praise
  • Security motive: desire for safety and stability
  • Curiosity motive: drive to explore and know

Nursing Relevance

  • Sick patients lose biological drives (anorexia, reduced thirst)
  • Nurse must recognize altered motives and adapt care plans
  • Social motives (affiliation, security) become prominent during illness
😤
Frustration & Conflicts
Unit III
▾

Frustration — Definition

  • The blocking or thwarting of goal-directed behaviour
  • Occurs when a motivated person is prevented from reaching their goal
  • Sources: External (people, situations) or Internal (personal limitations)

Responses to Frustration

  • Aggression: outward hostility (Dollard's Frustration-Aggression Hypothesis)
  • Withdrawal/Regression: retreating to earlier behaviours
  • Fixation: repeating ineffective behaviour
  • Resignation: giving up
  • Constructive coping: problem-solving, finding alternatives

Conflict — Definition

  • Simultaneous presence of two or more incompatible motives or goals
  • Creates psychological tension

Types of Conflict (Lewin)

  • Approach-Approach: choosing between TWO attractive goals (pleasant dilemma) e.g., two good job offers
  • Avoidance-Avoidance: choosing between TWO unpleasant alternatives (double bind) e.g., surgery vs pain
  • Approach-Avoidance: one goal has BOTH attractive and repulsive aspects e.g., wanting recovery but fearing surgery
  • Double Approach-Avoidance: multiple goals each with positive & negative aspects — most complex, most common in real life
⭐ Exam: Define all 4 types of conflict with examples. Patients often experience approach-avoidance conflict about treatment.
🔑 "AA, AA, AA, DAA" = App-App, Avoid-Avoid, App-Avoid, Double App-Avoid
🏔️
Maslow's Hierarchy & Self-Actualization
Unit III
▾

Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs (1943)

  • Level 1 — Physiological: food, water, sleep, warmth, oxygen, homeostasis (MOST basic)
  • Level 2 — Safety/Security: physical safety, stability, freedom from fear
  • Level 3 — Love/Belonging: social connections, relationships, affection, sense of belonging
  • Level 4 — Esteem: self-esteem, achievement, recognition, mastery, respect
  • Level 5 — Self-Actualization: realizing full potential, creativity, peak experiences

Self-Actualization

  • Highest level — becoming the best one can be
  • Characteristics of self-actualized people (Maslow): realistic, problem-centered, autonomous, creative, spontaneous, deep relationships, peak experiences, acceptance of self & others
  • Only ~1% of people reach self-actualization
  • Examples: Gandhi, Einstein, Mother Teresa

Nursing Application

  • Sick patients often regress to lower levels (safety, physiological)
  • Nurse must meet lower needs FIRST before higher needs can be addressed
  • Sickness threatens safety and esteem needs
  • Recovery path = ascending Maslow's pyramid
🔑 "Please Stop Loving Everyone Selflessly" — Physiological, Safety, Love, Esteem, Self-actualization
⭐ Must draw and label the pyramid. Know how each level applies to patient care.
Unit IV

Emotions

▾
💓
Definition & Nature of Emotions
Unit IV
▾

Definition

  • Emotion = complex feeling states accompanied by physiological arousal and behavioural expressions that influence behaviour
  • From Latin emovere = to stir up, to move
  • Components: Subjective feeling + Physiological changes + Cognitive appraisal + Behavioural expression

Basic / Primary Emotions (Paul Ekman)

  • Fear, Anger, Disgust, Happiness, Sadness, Surprise — universal across cultures
  • Also: Contempt added later

Theories of Emotion

  • James-Lange Theory: physiological change → emotion ("we feel afraid because we tremble")
  • Cannon-Bard Theory: stimulus → simultaneous physiology + emotion (thalamus theory)
  • Schachter-Singer (Two-Factor): physiological arousal + cognitive label = emotion
  • Lazarus Cognitive Appraisal: emotion depends on how you appraise/interpret the situation

Physiological Changes in Emotion

  • ↑ Heart rate, ↑ Blood pressure
  • Pupil dilation, sweating, piloerection
  • Release of adrenaline (epinephrine) from adrenal medulla
  • Controlled by Autonomic Nervous System (sympathetic activation)
  • Limbic system (amygdala) plays key role in emotional processing
🔑 FACE-SP: Fear, Anger, Contempt, Excitement, Sadness, Pride — common emotions in sick patients
😷
Emotions in Sickness & Nursing
Unit IV
▾

How Sickness Affects Emotions

  • Anxiety & Fear: most common; fear of death, pain, disability, unknown
  • Anger: at situation, caregivers, God ("Why me?"); may be displaced onto nurses
  • Depression/Sadness: loss of role, independence, body image changes
  • Denial: refusing to accept diagnosis (especially cancer, AIDS)
  • Guilt: feeling they caused their illness
  • Hope: crucial positive emotion for recovery

Kübler-Ross Stages of Grief (DABDA)

  • Denial → Anger → Bargaining → Depression → Acceptance
  • Applied to any major loss: terminal illness, amputation, chronic disease

How Emotions Affect Physical Health

  • Anxiety → ↑ cortisol → immunosuppression
  • Chronic stress → cardiovascular disease, GI disorders, skin problems
  • Psychosomatic disorders: real physical symptoms caused/worsened by emotions

Nursing Management

  • Active listening, empathy, therapeutic communication
  • Explain procedures to reduce fear
  • Allow expression of emotions without judgment
  • Maintain patient dignity — prevents shame and humiliation
  • Refer to counsellor/psychiatrist when needed
⭐ Know DABDA stages and apply them to patients with terminal illness. Very frequently asked in nursing exams.
Unit V

Personality

▾
🎭
Definition & Constituents of Personality
Unit V
▾

Definition

  • Personality = the unique, relatively stable pattern of thoughts, emotions, and behaviours that characterize an individual and distinguish them from others
  • From Latin persona = mask (used by Greek actors)
  • Gordon Allport: "the dynamic organization within the individual of those psychophysical systems that determine his unique adjustments to the environment"

Constituents of Personality

  • Traits: stable, consistent characteristics (e.g., introversion, conscientiousness)
  • Self-concept: how one perceives oneself
  • Values & Beliefs: guiding principles
  • Temperament: biologically based emotional reactivity (born with)
  • Character: moral aspects of personality (shaped by learning)
  • Habits: repeated patterns of behaviour

Major Personality Theories

  • Freud's Psychoanalytic: Id (pleasure principle), Ego (reality principle), Superego (moral principle)
  • Big Five (OCEAN): Openness, Conscientiousness, Extraversion, Agreeableness, Neuroticism
  • Type A/B: Type A = competitive, time-pressured, hostile (↑ MI risk); Type B = relaxed
  • Eysenck: Introversion-Extraversion + Neuroticism-Stability dimensions

Freud's Structural Theory

  • Id: primitive, unconscious, seeks immediate pleasure (newborn is all id)
  • Ego: conscious mediator between id and reality — delays gratification
  • Superego: internalized morality, conscience, ideal self
🔑 OCEAN for Big Five; IES for Freud's Id-Ego-Superego
🏥
Personality in Sickness & Nursing
Unit V
▾

How Sickness Affects Personality

  • Regression: return to childlike dependence
  • Increased irritability, mood swings
  • Changes in self-concept (especially with disability, disfigurement)
  • Loss of role identity (breadwinner, parent)
  • Introversion may increase with chronic illness

Personality Types & Response to Illness

  • Type A: poor patient — resists dependence, hostile, impatient; hard to nurse
  • Type B: better patient — compliant, relaxed
  • Neurotic: tends to exaggerate symptoms, more anxious
  • Paranoid: suspicious of nurses, non-compliant

Nurse's Personality — Qualities Needed

  • Empathy, patience, emotional stability
  • Good communication, adaptability
  • Non-judgmental attitude
  • High emotional intelligence (EQ)
  • Self-awareness — knows own reactions/biases

Nurse-Patient Relationship & Personality

  • Nurse's personality directly affects therapeutic relationship
  • Warm, stable nurse personality → patient trust → better compliance → better outcomes
  • Burnout affects nurse's personality — self-care is essential
⭐ Question: "How does personality affect nursing care?" Answer: Both patient's and nurse's personality influence therapeutic relationship and recovery.
Unit VI

Psychological Aspects of Nursing

▾
🩺
Behaviour & Sickness
Unit VI
▾

How Sickness Changes Behaviour

  • Regression: dependent, demanding behaviour
  • Sick role (Talcott Parsons): exempt from duties, expected to seek help and get well
  • Non-compliance: refusing medications, diet, rest
  • Changed communication patterns (withdrawal, aggression)
  • Illness behaviour: actions taken to define one's illness and seek remedy

Factors Affecting Behaviour in Sickness

  • Nature and severity of illness
  • Pre-morbid personality
  • Cultural and religious beliefs
  • Family support system
  • Previous hospital experiences
  • Age and developmental level

Nurse's Role

  • Understand that difficult patient behaviour is often a response to illness/fear
  • Use therapeutic communication to address behavioural changes
  • Collaborate with multidisciplinary team
  • Set clear, consistent boundaries with compassion
👶
Psychological Needs of Children & Adolescents
Unit VI
▾

Children (0-12 years)

  • Separation anxiety: greatest fear — being separated from parents; allow rooming-in
  • Fear of procedures/pain: use play therapy, child-friendly explanations
  • Need for security: consistent caregivers, familiar toys, routines
  • Communication: use simple language, drawings, puppets
  • Regression: may revert to bedwetting, thumb-sucking — normal in illness
  • Erikson: Infants — Trust vs Mistrust; Toddlers — Autonomy vs Shame; School age — Industry vs Inferiority

Adolescents (12-18 years)

  • Identity vs Role confusion (Erikson stage)
  • Body image concerns: very sensitive to physical changes (scars, hair loss, weight changes)
  • Peer group importance: fear of being "different"
  • Privacy and dignity: must maintain; involve in decisions
  • Independence: explain rationale for all procedures; do not patronize
  • Sexuality concerns may arise with hospitalization

Nursing Approach

  • Children: involve parents, use play therapy, minimize separation
  • Adolescents: maintain confidentiality, peer support, treat as adult-in-training
👨
Psychological Needs of Adults
Unit VI
▾

Key Psychological Needs

  • Autonomy & Control: involve in treatment decisions; informed consent is essential
  • Role maintenance: worried about work, family responsibilities
  • Financial concerns: cost of illness causes anxiety — social worker involvement
  • Information needs: want to understand diagnosis, prognosis, treatment options
  • Intimacy/sexuality: may be affected by illness — address sensitively
  • Self-esteem: maintain dignity; avoid infantilizing (treating like child)

Erikson's Adult Stages

  • Young adult: Intimacy vs Isolation
  • Middle adult: Generativity vs Stagnation

Nursing Approach

  • Shared decision-making
  • Provide clear information, written materials
  • Respect cultural and religious beliefs
  • Maintain professional boundaries while being empathetic
👴
Psychological Needs of the Aged
Unit VI
▾

Erikson's Stage: Ego Integrity vs Despair

  • Successful aging = accepting life with satisfaction (ego integrity)
  • Failure = regret, bitterness (despair)

Psychological Challenges of Old Age

  • Fear of death and dying
  • Loneliness and social isolation
  • Cognitive decline (memory loss, dementia)
  • Loss of independence — very distressing
  • Sensory losses (vision, hearing) → frustration, withdrawal
  • Loss of spouse, friends — grief
  • Ageism — feeling disrespected/invisible

Nursing Approach

  • Speak clearly (not loudly!), face-to-face, simple sentences
  • Maintain their independence as much as possible
  • Encourage reminiscence therapy
  • Involve family; assess caregiver burden
  • Prevent falls, pressure sores (decreased pain sensation)
  • Respect experience and wisdom; avoid ageist language
👨‍👩‍👧
Psychological Needs of Attendants & Chronically Ill
Unit VI
▾

Psychological Needs of Attendants/Bystanders

  • Anxiety and fear about patient's condition
  • Need for accurate, timely information
  • May experience vicarious trauma (especially in ICU, critical care)
  • Caregiver burden — physical and emotional exhaustion
  • Financial stress from prolonged hospitalization
  • Guilt (especially if they feel responsible for accident/illness)

Nurse's Role with Attendants

  • Regular updates; designate spokesperson in large families
  • Prepare them psychologically for bad news
  • Teach caregiving skills to reduce anxiety
  • Acknowledge their emotional needs
  • Social worker/counselor referral for severe distress

Chronically Ill Individual

  • Depression and hopelessness common
  • Loss of purpose and meaning
  • Sick role becomes permanent — identity shifts to "patient"
  • Social withdrawal due to disability/pain
  • Non-compliance increases as hope diminishes
  • Spiritual needs intensify

Nursing Approach for Chronically Ill

  • Set realistic short-term goals to maintain hope
  • Empower patient: self-management skills, peer support groups
  • Palliative care approach: quality of life focus
  • Screen for depression regularly
Unit VII

Individual Differences

▾
🌈
Significance of Individual Differences
Unit VII
▾

Definition

  • Individual differences = variations in physical, psychological, social, and intellectual characteristics that make each person unique
  • No two individuals (even identical twins) are psychologically identical

Areas of Individual Differences

  • Intelligence, personality, aptitude, achievement
  • Attitudes, values, beliefs
  • Physical traits (height, weight, body structure)
  • Emotional reactivity, temperament
  • Learning style, pace, preferences
  • Coping styles and response to stress

Significance

  • Explains why people behave differently in similar situations
  • Basis for individualized patient care
  • Relevant to educational planning (nursing students learn differently)
  • Basis for psychological testing and assessment
🧬
Heredity vs Environment (Nature vs Nurture)
Unit VII
▾

Heredity (Nature)

  • Genetic transmission of traits from parents to offspring via chromosomes
  • Determines: physical traits, basic temperament, potential IQ range, susceptibility to some disorders
  • Evidence: twin studies (identical twins more similar even when raised apart)
  • Heritability of intelligence: ~50% genetic basis

Environment (Nurture)

  • All external influences: family, culture, education, nutrition, socioeconomic status
  • Prenatal environment: maternal nutrition, substance use, infections
  • Postnatal: parenting, peer influence, schooling, life experiences
  • Critically sensitive periods: early childhood (0-5 years) — most plastic

Interactionist View (Modern Consensus)

  • Both nature AND nurture interact continuously
  • Gene-environment interaction: genes may be expressed differently in different environments
  • Epigenetics: environment can switch genes on/off
  • Example: Genetically predisposed to depression — stressful environment triggers it; supportive environment may prevent it
⭐ Nursing relevance: Understanding heredity + environment helps assess disease risk, plan preventive care, and understand patient behaviour.
⚕️
Individual Differences in Health, Sickness & Nursing
Unit VII
▾

In Health

  • Pain threshold varies widely between individuals
  • Health-seeking behaviour differs by personality, culture, education
  • Lifestyle choices (diet, exercise) vary by individual differences
  • Health literacy — affects understanding of health information

In Sickness

  • Symptom perception varies (some ignore, some catastrophize)
  • Response to treatment (drug metabolism differences — pharmacogenomics)
  • Coping style in illness (active vs avoidant)
  • Speed of recovery varies

Implications in Nursing

  • Individualized care plans: not one-size-fits-all
  • Communication adaptation: adjust based on education, age, culture
  • Pain assessment: use validated tools; don't rely on stereotypes
  • Health education: tailor content, literacy level, language
  • Cultural competence: be aware of cultural differences in illness perception
  • Non-judgmental: differences are normal, not moral failures
Unit VIII

Intelligence & Abilities

▾
💡
Definition & Theories of Intelligence
Unit VIII
▾

Definition

  • Intelligence = the ability to learn from experience, solve problems, and adapt to new situations
  • Wechsler: "the global capacity of the individual to act purposefully, think rationally, and deal effectively with the environment"
  • Binet: the ability to judge, comprehend, and reason well

Theories of Intelligence

  • Spearman's Two-Factor Theory: General intelligence (g) + Specific abilities (s)
  • Thurstone's Primary Mental Abilities: 7 abilities: Verbal, Number, Spatial, Memory, Reasoning, Word Fluency, Perceptual Speed
  • Gardner's Multiple Intelligences (1983): 8 types: Linguistic, Logical-mathematical, Spatial, Musical, Bodily-kinesthetic, Interpersonal, Intrapersonal, Naturalist
  • Sternberg's Triarchic Theory: Analytical (academic), Creative, Practical (real-world)
  • Emotional Intelligence (EQ - Goleman): Self-awareness, Self-regulation, Empathy, Motivation, Social skills — highly relevant to nursing!

Intelligence Quotient (IQ)

  • IQ = (Mental Age / Chronological Age) × 100
  • Average IQ = 90-110; Average range = 85-115
  • Gifted = IQ > 130; Intellectual Disability = IQ < 70
🔑 IQ formula: MA/CA × 100 — Mental Age divided by Chronological Age × 100
📊
Measurement of Intelligence & Intelligence in Sickness
Unit VIII
▾

Intelligence Tests

  • Stanford-Binet Intelligence Scale: oldest; verbal emphasis; gives mental age
  • Wechsler Scales: WAIS (adults), WISC (children), WPPSI (preschool) — most widely used clinically; gives Verbal + Performance IQ
  • Raven's Progressive Matrices: non-verbal, culture-fair; pattern completion
  • Bhatia's Battery (India): culturally adapted, uses Kohs Blocks, Pass-along test

Types of Tests

  • Individual vs Group tests
  • Verbal vs Non-verbal vs Performance tests
  • Speed vs Power tests

Intelligence During Sickness

  • Acute illness: temporary cognitive decline (confusion, delirium)
  • Chronic illness: fatigue, medication effects reduce performance
  • Brain injury: specific cognitive deficits
  • Dementia: progressive deterioration of intelligence
  • Malnutrition/anemia: cognitive impairment especially in children

Nursing Implications

  • Assess cognitive function (use MMSE - Mini Mental State Examination)
  • Adjust teaching methods for intellectual level
  • Never assume fixed intelligence — illness temporarily depresses it
  • Special care for intellectually disabled patients
⭐ Know the key intelligence tests by name. WAIS is most commonly used in clinical settings for adults.
Unit IX

Learning

▾
📚
Definition & Conditions of Learning
Unit IX
▾

Definition

  • Learning = a relatively permanent change in behaviour (or potential for behaviour) that results from practice or experience
  • Key word: "relatively permanent" (distinguishes from fatigue, drugs, maturation)
  • Must result from experience (not biological maturation alone)

Types of Learning

  • Classical Conditioning (Pavlov): learning through association (NS + UCS → CS → CR)
  • Operant Conditioning (Skinner): learning through consequences (reinforcement, punishment)
  • Observational/Social Learning (Bandura): learning by watching others (modelling)
  • Insight Learning (Köhler): sudden understanding — "aha!" moment
  • Cognitive Learning: learning through thought, mental maps

Conditions of Learning

  • Readiness: physical and mental preparedness to learn
  • Motivation: desire to learn; intrinsic > extrinsic
  • Practice: repetition strengthens learning
  • Feedback/Knowledge of results: knowing if one is correct
  • Active participation: doing > passive listening
  • Meaningful material: learning is better when it has purpose
  • Appropriate environment: free from distractions, comfortable
🔑 RM-PFAM: Readiness, Motivation, Practice, Feedback, Active participation, Meaning
⚖️
Laws of Learning (Thorndike)
Unit IX
▾

Thorndike's Primary Laws

  • Law of Readiness: learning is more effective when learner is ready and willing; forcing learning on unprepared person causes frustration
  • Law of Exercise/Use: connections are strengthened by use (practice) and weakened by disuse; "use it or lose it"
  • Law of Effect: behaviours followed by pleasant consequences (satisfiers) are strengthened; behaviours followed by unpleasant consequences (annoyers) are weakened; basis of reinforcement theory

Thorndike's Secondary Laws

  • Law of Multiple Response: organism tries different responses until one works
  • Law of Set/Attitude: learner's mental set influences learning
  • Law of Partial Activity: selective response to relevant elements
  • Law of Analogy: responds to new situations based on past similar situations
  • Law of Associative Shift: any stimulus that occurs with a response can eventually elicit it

Application in Nursing Education

  • Assess patient's readiness before health teaching
  • Repeat key instructions (law of exercise)
  • Provide positive reinforcement for compliance (law of effect)
  • Use return demonstration — active practice
⭐ Thorndike's 3 primary laws are frequently asked: Readiness, Exercise, Effect — REE

Learning During Health & Sickness
Unit IX
▾

Learning in Health

  • Optimal when all conditions met (readiness, motivation, environment)
  • Health education possible at any time
  • Best time: patient is interested, not distracted

Learning During Sickness — Challenges

  • Pain and discomfort reduce concentration
  • Fever, medications (sedatives, analgesics) impair cognition
  • Anxiety interferes with encoding new information
  • Depression reduces motivation to learn
  • Fatigue limits attention span
  • Hospital environment — unfamiliar, noisy, distressing

Nursing Strategies for Learning in Sick Patients

  • Teach when patient is most alert (usually morning, after rest)
  • Keep sessions SHORT — 10-15 minutes maximum
  • Repeat important points multiple times
  • Use visual aids, pamphlets, models
  • Involve family as learning partners
  • Assess readiness; don't teach when in acute distress
  • Return demonstration — confirms learning
  • Follow up: re-teach if needed
Unit X

Memory & Forgetting

▾
🧠
Memory — Definition & Types
Unit X
▾

Definition

  • Memory = the mental capacity to encode, store, and retrieve information
  • Memory is the foundation of learning — without memory, nothing can be learned

Three Stages of Memory (Atkinson-Shiffrin Modal Model)

  • Encoding: converting information into a form the brain can process (acoustic, visual, semantic)
  • Storage: retaining encoded information over time
  • Retrieval: accessing stored information when needed

Types of Memory (Duration)

  • Sensory Memory: very brief (0.5-3 sec); holds exact copy of sensory input (iconic-visual, echoic-auditory)
  • Short-term Memory (STM) / Working Memory: 15-30 seconds; capacity = 7±2 chunks (Miller's Magic Number)
  • Long-term Memory (LTM): potentially unlimited capacity and duration

Types of Long-term Memory

  • Explicit (Declarative): conscious recall
    • Episodic: personal events ("my first day in hospital")
    • Semantic: general knowledge and facts
  • Implicit (Non-declarative): unconscious
    • Procedural: skills and habits (riding a bike, giving injection)
    • Priming: prior exposure influences later response
🔑 ESR = Encode, Store, Retrieve (memory stages)
SEL = Sensory, Explicit, Learning (LTM types)
❌
Forgetting — Definition, Theories & Sickness
Unit X
▾

Definition

  • Forgetting = the inability to recall or recognize information that was previously learned
  • Ebbinghaus Forgetting Curve: most forgetting (60-70%) occurs within the FIRST 24 hours
  • Spaced practice (distributed learning) reduces forgetting

Theories of Forgetting

  • Decay Theory: memory trace fades over time with disuse (like an unused path)
  • Interference Theory: other memories interfere
    • Proactive interference: old learning interferes with new
    • Retroactive interference: new learning interferes with old
  • Motivated Forgetting/Repression: unconscious suppression of painful memories (Freud)
  • Retrieval Failure: memory exists but cannot be accessed (tip-of-tongue phenomenon)
  • Encoding Failure: information never properly stored in first place

Memory & Forgetting in Sickness

  • Amnesia: memory loss
    • Anterograde: cannot form NEW memories (after injury)
    • Retrograde: cannot recall events BEFORE injury
  • Dementia: progressive memory loss (Alzheimer's)
  • Delirium: acute confusional state — impaired memory and attention
  • Medications (benzodiazepines, antihistamines): impair encoding
  • Depression: pseudodementia — impairs recall but not storage
  • High fever: impairs memory consolidation

Nursing Implications

  • Use written instructions (don't rely only on verbal)
  • Repeat key information at each visit
  • Use memory aids: pill organisers, reminder cards
  • Involve caregiver for patients with memory impairment
  • Assess memory with tools: MMSE, clock drawing test
⭐ Anterograde vs Retrograde amnesia — know the difference. Anterograde = AFTER injury (can't make new memories). Retrograde = BEFORE injury (lost old memories).

Attitudes

▾
💬
Definition, Development & Modification of Attitudes
Unit XI
▾

Definition

  • Attitude = a learned, relatively enduring predisposition to respond in a consistently favourable or unfavourable manner to a person, object, or situation
  • Gordon Allport: "A mental and neural state of readiness to respond"

Three Components (ABC Model)

  • A — Affective (Emotional): feelings associated with the object ("I fear injections")
  • B — Behavioural (Conative): predisposition to act in certain way ("I avoid injections")
  • C — Cognitive: beliefs and knowledge about the object ("Injections cause pain")

Development of Attitudes

  • Classical Conditioning: associations formed in childhood
  • Operant Conditioning: attitudes reinforced by rewards/punishments
  • Modelling/Observational Learning: learning attitudes by watching parents, peers, media
  • Direct experience: personal encounters shape attitudes
  • Cultural transmission: family, religion, education, media

Modification of Attitudes

  • Persuasive communication: credible source + strong message + right audience
  • Cognitive Dissonance (Festinger): holding contradictory beliefs causes discomfort → attitude change
  • Exposure effect: repeated exposure often increases liking
  • Group influence: conformity to group norms
  • Education and information: correcting misconceptions
🔑 ABC = Affective, Behavioural, Cognitive — components of attitude
🏥
Attitudes in Health & Sickness
Unit XI
▾

Attitudes Affecting Health Behaviour

  • Positive health attitudes → preventive behaviour (vaccination, screening, exercise)
  • Negative attitudes toward hospitals → delayed treatment-seeking
  • Stigma attitudes → reluctance to seek mental health treatment
  • Cultural attitudes (e.g., "illness is God's will") → fatalism, non-compliance

Health Belief Model (Rosenstock)

  • Perceived susceptibility: "Am I at risk?"
  • Perceived severity: "How serious is it?"
  • Perceived benefits: "Will treatment help?"
  • Perceived barriers: "What will it cost me?"
  • Cues to action: triggers that prompt health behaviour

Nurse's Attitudes in Care

  • Nurse attitudes toward patients directly affect quality of care
  • Negative attitudes (ageism, sexism, stigma toward addiction/HIV) = barrier to care
  • Empathic, non-judgmental attitude = therapeutic
  • Self-reflection and awareness needed to identify own biases

Attitude Change in Patients

  • Building trust before attempting attitude change
  • Motivational Interviewing (MI) — effective for resistant patients
  • Use patient's own values to motivate change
  • Peer educators/patient groups — very effective
⭐ Attitude ≠ behaviour. Knowing something is healthy doesn't mean a person will do it. Address barriers to translate attitude → behaviour.

Mental Hygiene & Mental Health
Unit XII
▾

Mental Hygiene — Definition

  • Mental hygiene = the science of maintaining and promoting mental health and preventing mental disorders
  • Term coined by Clifford Beers (1908) in "A Mind That Found Itself"
  • Focuses on prevention at three levels: Primary (prevent), Secondary (early detect), Tertiary (rehabilitation)

Mental Health — Definition

  • WHO: "a state of wellbeing in which the individual realizes their own abilities, can cope with normal stresses of life, can work productively, and is able to make a contribution to their community"
  • Mental health is NOT merely the absence of mental illness
  • Positive mental health = flourishing, not just surviving

Characteristics of a Mentally Healthy Person

  • Positive self-concept and self-esteem
  • Reality-oriented — perceives self and world accurately
  • Effective coping with stress and frustration
  • Satisfying interpersonal relationships
  • Emotional balance — feels full range of emotions appropriately
  • Autonomous — can make independent decisions
  • Productive — contributes to self and society
  • Flexible and adaptable to change
  • Free from excessive anxiety, conflict, guilt
  • Spiritual wellbeing — sense of meaning and purpose
🔑 PREFACE: Positive self, Reality, Effective coping, Friends/relationships, Autonomy, Contributes, Emotional balance

Defense Mechanisms — Complete Guide
Unit XII
▾

Definition

  • Defense mechanisms = unconscious psychological strategies used by the ego to protect itself from anxiety and distress caused by unacceptable thoughts, feelings, or conflicts (Freud)
  • They are automatic, unconscious, not deliberate lying
  • Healthy in moderation; pathological when excessive or rigid

Mature Defense Mechanisms (Healthy)

  • Sublimation: redirecting unacceptable impulses into socially acceptable activities (e.g., aggressive person becomes surgeon)
  • Humour: using comedy to deal with anxiety
  • Altruism: helping others to reduce own distress
  • Suppression: consciously postponing awareness of stressor

Neurotic Defense Mechanisms (Common)

  • Repression: pushing painful memories/desires into unconscious (basis of all defenses) — e.g., forgetting traumatic event
  • Rationalization: giving logical but untrue reasons for behaviour ("sour grapes") — e.g., "I failed because the questions were unfair"
  • Projection: attributing own unacceptable feelings to others — e.g., "I'm not angry, HE is angry at ME"
  • Reaction Formation: expressing opposite of true feeling — e.g., hating someone but acting overly friendly
  • Displacement: redirecting feelings to a safer target — e.g., angry at boss → yells at family
  • Intellectualization: dealing with emotions through abstract thinking — e.g., cancer patient reads research instead of processing fear
  • Denial: refusing to acknowledge painful reality — e.g., alcoholic says "I don't have a drinking problem"

Immature Defense Mechanisms

  • Regression: reverting to earlier developmental behaviour under stress — e.g., adult throws tantrum; sick child resumes bedwetting
  • Somatization: converting emotional distress into physical symptoms
  • Splitting: seeing things as all good or all bad (black/white thinking)
  • Acting out: expressing feelings through actions rather than words

Nursing Relevance

  • Recognize patient's defense mechanisms without judgment
  • Denial in newly diagnosed patient — don't force acceptance; work with it
  • Projection — patient accusing nurse of not caring — understand it's displaced anger
  • Regression in sick adults and children — reassure and support
  • Document behaviour, not interpretation (e.g., "patient refused to discuss diagnosis" not "patient is in denial")

Principles of Mental Hygiene & Nurse's Role
Unit XII
▾

Principles of Mental Hygiene

  • Know yourself (self-understanding reduces inner conflicts)
  • Accept yourself (realistic self-appraisal)
  • Build and maintain satisfying relationships
  • Maintain balance between work and recreation
  • Develop healthy coping strategies for stress
  • Cultivate a sense of purpose and meaning
  • Seek help early — destigmatize mental health issues
  • Maintain physical health — mind-body connection

Nurse's Role in Mental Hygiene

  • Prevention: health education, stress management programs, suicide prevention
  • Early identification: screening patients for psychological distress
  • Therapeutic environment: create safe, supportive ward atmosphere
  • Emotional support: therapeutic communication, empathy, active listening
  • Referral: to psychiatrist, counsellor, social worker as appropriate
  • Self-care: nurse must model mental hygiene — avoid burnout
  • Stigma reduction: educate public and colleagues about mental health

Nurse's Own Mental Health

  • High-stress profession — increased risk of burnout, compassion fatigue, PTSD
  • Signs of burnout: emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, reduced personal accomplishment
  • Self-care strategies: exercise, social support, supervision, mindfulness, work-life balance
  • Seek help without stigma — nurses deserve mental health care too
